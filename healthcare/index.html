Why this is different

Most security testers have never seen a hospital run.

They will scan your systems competently and hand you a report full of findings that assume a software company's workflow. Then they will recommend something operationally impossible, like disabling a shared account that four shifts depend on, and the report goes in a drawer.

A finding nobody can act on is a finding written badly. The working rule behind every report I deliver

I write remediation that accounts for how the ward actually works — what can change on Monday, what needs the vendor, and what needs a process change before any technical fix will hold.

The register

What I look for, and why I know where to look.

These are the patterns that recur in clinical systems. Not theory — the things that are usually there.

01

Patient records reachable by changing a number

The report URL ends in an ID. Change the ID, get someone else's results. No login bypass needed — the system simply never checks whether this user should see this patient. The single most common serious flaw in health systems, and the easiest to test for.

02

Lab results on predictable links

Test result PDFs served from a path a patient can guess, with no authentication at all, because the link was meant to be convenient for people who cannot manage a password.

03

Shared clinical accounts

One login for the ward, the password on a note by the monitor, unchanged for years, still active for staff who left. The audit trail says the account did it, which means it says nothing.

04

The HIS the vendor installed and nobody has touched since

Running an unsupported version, on an operating system past end-of-life, with default administrative credentials that were never changed because nobody knew they existed.

05

Real patient data in the test environment

Because testing with realistic data was easier. The staging server has no monitoring, weaker credentials, and is often the only one reachable from the public internet.

06

Backups in public storage

A database dump placed in cloud storage for a migration and left there. Indexed, downloadable, and containing every record the system has ever held.

07

Credentials shipped to the browser

API keys and database strings inside the JavaScript your patient portal serves to anyone who opens it. This is my core specialism and it is present far more often than people expect.

08

Clinical data moving through personal messaging

Ward WhatsApp groups carrying patient photographs and results, because the official system is too slow to use during a shift. A process problem no scanner will ever find, and one I will raise because I know it is happening.

AI in clinical settings

If you have added a chatbot, the risk changed shape.

RETRIEVAL

It reads documents it did not write

An assistant answering from your policies, guidelines or patient notes will act on instructions hidden inside that content. The user never sees them. In your logs it looks like the patient asked.

TOOL ACCESS

It can do things, not just say things

Book, cancel, look up, message. If the rule about who may do what lives in the prompt rather than behind the tool, persuading the model is enough. No injection required.

CROSS-PATIENT

Context leaks between people

Where one model instance serves many patients, the question is whether anything from one conversation can surface in another. It is a question with a testable answer.

CLINICAL SAFETY

Confident wrong answers

A model that will not say "I don't know" is a clinical risk, not just a quality problem. I test what it does at the edge of its knowledge, and whether it escalates to a human when it should.

I build these systems as well as test them — an open-source gateway guardrail and an injection-hardened agent pipeline, both public on GitHub. I am not reselling somebody else's scanner output.

How testing works here

A ward system cannot go down. I know that.

This is where most assessments go wrong in a clinical setting. Testing that would be routine against a software product is unacceptable against a system somebody is using to check a drug dose.

  • Passive first. Most of what I find comes from what your systems already show the public internet — no requests that touch clinical workflow at all.
  • Rate-limited, and scheduled around you. Testing windows are agreed, blackout periods respected, and nothing runs during a handover or a known peak.
  • Never destructive. No load testing, nothing that could degrade availability, and no action that writes to or alters a record.
  • Real patient data is never extracted. Where a finding exposes it, I evidence that the exposure exists and stop. The data itself is not retained, and no clinical record is read beyond confirming the flaw is real.
  • One phone call stops everything. Any time, any reason, no explanation needed. Work halts within the hour.
  • Critical findings reach you the same day — not held back for the final report.

Before you ask

What I don't do.

I do not certify compliance.

I am not an auditor and I will not sign anything claiming your organisation meets HIPAA, HITRUST, ISO 27001 or any other standard. What I provide is a technical assessment with evidence, which is often what an auditor or an enterprise customer actually asks you for — but the certification itself is somebody else's signature.

I do not do full-scope penetration tests.

Network, physical and social-engineering engagements need a team and credentials I do not yet hold. I will tell you that rather than do it thinly, and where I can I will point you to someone who can.

I do not test anything without written permission.
